# Luma — research-grounded persona set

**SYNTHETIC ARCHETYPES, GROUNDED IN DESK RESEARCH. NOT USERS. NOT INTERVIEWED.**
No name below is a person. No line below is a quote. Nothing here may enter
`research/evidence-ledger.json` (ADR-024). The evidence ledger still reads zero, and that
number is still truthful.

Round 2 of the Luma persona work, replacing the 40-participant roster whose pain
assignments were a rotation lattice (ART-029). Built from open-source desk research across
forums, regulators, trade bodies, peer-reviewed work and news, run under explicit
bias controls. Sources are not published here by decision of Agentic Designer - RP.

## 1. What the research overturned

Four of the brief's premises did not survive contact with the sources. These matter more
than the persona set, because a persona built on a false premise is a false persona
however carefully it is written.

### 1.1 Planning is the pleasure. The pain is one specific sub-task.

The strongest disconfirming finding of the whole sweep, and it inverts the product thesis.
In enthusiast travel communities, planning-as-enjoyment is the **majority** position, not a
fringe: described as half the fun, a labour of love, experiencing the trip twice, and in one
case as a working professional's daily stress relief. Lead times of nine to eighteen months
are normal and are treated as the point, not as a cost.

Enjoyment and difficulty are **not opposites** in this data. The same people who call
planning a labour of love name one sub-task as miserable, and it is consistently the same
one: **multi-leg ground transport between cities on specific dates.** Discovery, comparing
and sequencing are the pleasure. Working out how to get from A to B is the pain.

> A product that "removes the stress of planning" would be destroying the part they came for.

**Caveat, stated because it is load-bearing:** roughly half the leisure material came from
one US forum whose community is older, affluent and self-selected for people who like
planning. The finding is real; its strength is partly an artefact of where non-leading
queries landed. It should be re-tested on a different population before it drives strategy.

### 1.2 The dreaded moment is the return leg, not the destination arrival.

The brief points at arrival-to-accommodation. Families point somewhere else entirely: the
**home** airport, late at night, with tired children and a car parked a thirty-minute bus
ride away. Destination arrival, for a family on a package with transfers included, is
routinely described as a non-event. Several buy a private transfer upgrade for about £100
and stop thinking about it.

### 1.3 The "needs help booking" persona is young, not old.

The current roster has one participant aged 50 and nobody older, on the implicit assumption
that older travellers struggle. Three independent instrument types say that assumption is
wrong as a default. Smartphone ownership among the over-65s runs near four in five;
over-65s are online more than three hours a day, mostly on a phone; and the over-70s who
travel present as *more* organised than average, building fifty-page trip books in paper
and electronic versions.

Meanwhile the rate of booking through a travel professional is **highest among 18–24s and
25–34s and among families with young children** — not among the over-55s. If the roster
needs someone who wants a human to do it, the evidence pins that to a twenty-two-year-old
or a young family.

**Hold both, though.** The variance *inside* the 55+ band is larger than the gap between it
and younger bands, and the split point sits nearer 75 than 55. Among over-50s already buying
holidays, only around two in five are happy to book online.

### 1.4 The failure mode is near-miss completion, not refusal.

The sharpest single case in the sweep: a 79-year-old and her husband **did** check in
online, printed the *return* boarding passes instead of the outbound pair, and were charged
£55 each at the desk. The airline's position was that they had failed to check in online.
They had not. They were digitally *almost* successful.

Design against the near-miss, not against the person who never starts.

### 1.5 For disabled travellers, research is a gate on booking at all.

Not a step inside the funnel — a gate before it. Around three in five will avoid a venue
that has not published access information, presuming it inaccessible. Around three-quarters
have found a venue's own website misleading or inaccurate, so published information is
treated as unreliable by default and verified **by telephone**. The canonical sequence is
shortlist on an aggregator, then ring the property, then ring the airline again 48 hours
out to confirm the assistance request actually stuck to the booking.

The abandonment this describes fires before any funnel a product team would instrument.

### 1.6 Nobody is asking for one app.

Contradicted from every segment independently. Frequent flyers run several search tools
deliberately and can explain the mechanism: different tools see different inventory.
Families evaluated purpose-built trip apps and split — one adopted a dedicated planner, one
damned another as buggy, several stayed on a spreadsheet or the notes app, and one rejected
the framing entirely because what she wanted was a *map that clusters activities to cut
walking*, which is a routing need, not an aggregation need.

The most-used sync mechanism found anywhere is forwarding a confirmation email to an
itinerary service — an aggregator that works precisely **because it does not try to own the
booking**.

## 2. Bias controls, and how they held

| Control | How it was implemented | Held? |
|---|---|---|
| Non-leading queries | Searched "how I planned my trip", never "trip planning is stressful" | Yes |
| Mandatory disconfirming searches | Three or more per segment, aimed at refuting the brief | Yes — and three of four returned strong counter-evidence |
| Demographic frame fixed first | Age, ability, language and household spread set before searching | Partly — see gaps |
| Source-type quota | Forums for voice, regulators and academics for under-represented segments | Partly — one forum dominates the leisure material |
| Co-occurrence, not rotation | Pains assigned from observed clustering, variable count per persona | Yes |
| Blind check | An agent that did not build the set attempts to reverse-engineer the rule | **NOT YET RUN** |

**Where the controls did not hold, stated plainly.** One US forum supplies roughly half the
leisure evidence and skews toward people who enjoy planning. The disability segment has
strong regulator and market numbers but **thin first-person voice** — most of what exists is
advice content, and advice must not be laundered into persona speech. Reddit was blocked by
policy throughout, removing the largest pool of unmoderated first-person accounts. Business
travellers are **incomplete**: that research agent was terminated by a spend limit mid-run.
Nothing outside the UK and US in any depth, and no non-anglophone material beyond one
translated academic study.

## 3. The persona set

Fourteen archetypes. Pain counts vary from one to five and cross segment boundaries freely,
because that is what the sources show. Three reject the product premise outright.

Ages: 19, 22, 24, 27, 31, 34, 38, 41, 45, 52, 58, 67, 74, 81. **Six are over 45 and three
are over 65** — the band the previous roster omitted entirely.

### The premise-rejectors

**P01 · Marianne, 67, retired solicitor, Yorkshire.** Plans nine to fifteen months out and
calls it the best part of the year. Keeps a spreadsheet with a row per day and colours a
cell green when a payment clears. Would find a product that "takes the planning off your
hands" actively unwelcome. **Her one real pain is ground transport** — working out which
regional train, which ticket type, on which date. *Pains: multi-leg ground transport.*

**P02 · Tomas, 45, structural engineer, Manchester.** Runs four flight search tools on
purpose and can tell you why: they surface different inventory. Has seen the same route
priced hundreds of pounds apart across two of them on the same parameters. Consolidation
reads to him as *seeing less*. *Pains: none he would name — he is a control case.*

**P03 · Aisling, 34, hospital pharmacist, Dublin.** Books a package precisely so she does
not have to think, and is happy. Values collective financial protection and one number to
call over any interface improvement. Would not download a planning app. *Pains: none in
planning; one in the return leg.*

### The designated planners

**P04 · Reuben, 52, quantity surveyor, Cardiff.** Organised twelve nights for eight people
across three generations. The stress had nothing to do with software: he was the only one
who knew everything, and was asked "what's the plan?" for weeks after circulating a
detailed itinerary. Afterwards, blamed for the pace and the cost. *Pains: group
coordination; being the single point of knowledge; post-trip resentment.*

**P05 · Nkechi, 38, teacher, Birmingham.** Keeps the family holiday spreadsheet that other
parents in her year group ring her about. The artefact has an audience beyond her own
household. *Pains: group coordination; activity release windows.*

### Families

**P06 · Siân, 41, part-time nurse, Swansea.** Two children, nine and five. Budget stated as
a single total for the whole trip, never a nightly rate. Books a package with transfers,
finds the destination arrival easy, and dreads the home airport at ten at night with a
thirty-minute bus to the car. *Pains: return leg; car-seat provision at destination.*

**P07 · Deniz, 31, warehouse supervisor, Leicester.** Toddler plus a five-year-old. Works to
the activity-slot release clock — six in the morning for the good pool times. Uses the notes
app, one note per location. *Pains: booking-window competition; on-site activity management.*

**P08 · Fabio, 27, chef, Naples.** Travels to family with a baby. Everything bends around
feeds and sleep. Books late because his shifts move. *Pains: schedule volatility; arrival
with an infant.*

### Disabled and assisted travellers

*Written from regulator and market evidence plus advice literature. **The register here is
the least grounded in the set** and should not be treated as voice until first-person
material is collected.*

**P09 · Halina, 58, powered wheelchair user, Kraków.** Will not book until under-bed
clearance is confirmed in centimetres, because a hoist has to fit. Asks the hotel for
photographs of the room, bathroom and entrance. Rings the airline 48 hours out to check the
assistance request survived the booking. Photographs her chair from every angle at handover.
*Pains: unpublished access information; assistance ending before the journey does; equipment
damage risk; verification burden.*

**P10 · Oskar, 24, autistic, travels alone, Gothenburg.** Two different hearing-protection
devices for two different noise problems. Arrives two hours early to remove time pressure.
Downloads everything offline in advance. *Pains: sensory load; unpredictability; time
pressure.*

**P11 · Ruth, 74, low vision, Leeds.** Uses a remote sighted-assistance service to read
signage and find gates, because the airport does not solve it. Confident with the two apps
she uses and uninterested in a third. *Pains: wayfinding; signage; assistance reliability.*

### First-timers and the near-miss

**P12 · Jaden, 19, apprentice, Plymouth.** First flight. His actual questions were mechanical,
not emotional: does my bag go through, where do I find the gate, is passport control the
same as immigration. Booked through an agency because his college recommended one.
*Pains: airport process opacity; document uncertainty.*

**P13 · Priya, 22, student, London.** Books through a travel professional — the single
likeliest group to do so. Wants someone accountable if it breaks, not a better interface.
*Pains: trust; recourse when something fails.*

**P14 · Bernard, 81, retired, Ealing.** Fifty years of travel, still walks fifteen thousand
steps a day, builds his own trip books. Books online independently. **Printed the wrong pair
of boarding passes once and paid £110 at the desk.** He is not digitally excluded; he was
digitally almost successful. *Pains: near-miss completion; interfaces that punish a single
misread; connection anxiety, so nonstop only.*

## 5. What remains, and what this cannot claim

**Not done.** The interviews have not been regenerated. Twelve to sixteen at genuine depth
is the agreed next step, split across Script A and the 60-minute Script B, which has never
been run once. The blind check has not been run: an agent that did not build these personas
must try to reverse-engineer the assignment rule, exactly as the rotation was caught in
round 1. Until it does, this set is unaudited.

**Cannot claim.** The registered type for `persona` says "Evidence-backed archetype. Never
composite fiction." These are composites grounded in desk research, not archetypes derived
from primary research with users. That is a real tension with the contract and it is
reported rather than dodged — the same collision ART-029 found between its own type and
ADR-024.

**Structurally impossible from desk research.** Every source here is typed and edited prose.
Forum posts are performances for peers; academic quotes are translated and tidied. Nothing
in this sweep contains hesitation, self-repair, or a person working something out aloud. The
disfluency gap that makes round 1 read as synthetic **cannot be closed by more desk
research**. It needs recordings, or it needs to be generated deliberately and labelled as
generated.
